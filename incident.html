The first hour after a suspected compromise

Hacked account, ransomware note, strange logins, a device acting wrong. What you do in the first hour often decides how much can be understood and recovered later. This is general guidance, not a substitute for advice about your specific situation, and it isn't legal advice.

Do this

  • Disconnect affected devices from the network (unplug the cable or turn off Wi-Fi). Isolating is usually safer than powering off.
  • Photograph or screenshot ransom notes, error messages, and odd pop-ups.
  • Write down what you noticed, when, and what you did. Times matter.
  • From a clean device, change passwords and turn on multi-factor authentication for important accounts, such as email and banking.
  • Tell whoever manages your IT, your insurer, and your attorney if your situation calls for it.

Avoid this

  • Don't wipe, reinstall, or factory-reset the device. That can destroy the evidence that explains what happened.
  • Don't keep using the affected computer for normal work.
  • Don't run random "cleaner" tools or download fixes from pop-ups.
  • Don't pay a ransom or reply to an attacker before getting advice.
  • Don't plug backup drives into an infected system. They can be encrypted too.

Next few hours

  1. List the affected devices, accounts, and data, and what each one is used for.
  2. Check whether your backups are intact and disconnected from the affected systems.
  3. Decide who needs to know, including customers, staff, or regulators if personal data may be involved. Requirements vary, so ask your attorney.
  4. Keep affected devices powered and untouched where practical until you've discussed preservation.

What an investigation can and can't tell you

A careful examination can often reconstruct how access happened and what was touched. It can't always, and results depend on the condition and age of the evidence. Findings should state what is supported, what is uncertain, and what comes next. Recovery isn't guaranteed either.
